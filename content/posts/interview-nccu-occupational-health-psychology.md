# Interview: what it is like to work as a doctor in Malaysia

**Engagement:** Interview subject for a masters assignment in Occupational and Health Psychology
**Institution:** Department of Business Administration, National Cheng Chi University (NCCU), Taiwan
**Interviewer:** Izzat Zaid and coursemates
**Format:** Zoom, 11 November 2026, about one hour

---

## The engagement

On 11 November 2026, Dr. Syamirul spent an hour on Zoom with Izzat Zaid and her coursemates at National Cheng Chi University in Taiwan. They are studying Occupational and Health Psychology under the university's Department of Business Administration, and their assignment asked them to interview a working professional about the demands of the job, its effect on health, and what a workplace does to support its people. They chose a medical doctor, and Izzat, whom Dr. Syamirul knows from the ADHD community in Malaysia, asked him.

This kind of work sits alongside DRMSR's consultancy: a clinician who has worked in government hospitals, private hospitals, GP clinics and digital health, speaking plainly to researchers about what the job does to the people who do it. The interview followed six prepared questions. What follows is a summary of his answers.

## A doctor's working day, in his case

Dr. Syamirul graduated from Nizhny Novgorod State Medical Academy in Russia in 2012. He served seven years in Malaysian government service, two as a house officer and five as a medical officer, then moved to private practice: a year as a resident general practitioner in Kedah, four years as an emergency department medical officer at a private hospital in the Klang Valley, and from 2022 digital health, first as a full-time engineer at a startup and since 2026 running DRMSR. He still practises as a freelance locum doctor, one or two days a week, in shifts of six to ten hours.

His regular clinic is a small GP practice near an industrial area, where half to seventy percent of patients are foreign workers and the rest are general cases. It sees about one patient an hour. By comparison, a doctor in a government health clinic sees five to ten an hour, and early in his private career he worked a clinic that saw more than a hundred and twenty patients between eight in the morning and ten at night. He chooses the light clinic deliberately: it gives him the time to take a history, examine properly, and talk to the patient.

## The three demands

He named three things a doctor in private practice is managing at once.

**Safety.** The first duty is a safe diagnosis and a safe management plan, whatever the queue looks like. In a clinic seeing a hundred and eighty patients a day, every consultation is rushed: there is no time to probe the history or examine fully, and the doctor's ability to practise safely is compromised.

**Time.** Most Malaysian GP clinics run on a walk-in queue, so the doctor's time per patient is set by how many people are waiting. Some hospitals, and GP systems in countries like Australia, book patients into fixed slots, which lets the doctor give the whole slot to one person. Without that, the doctor has to wrap up and move on.

**Expectations.** A GP clinic sees everything. Some patients arrive with chest pain or a heart attack because they did not want to go to the hospital, and the doctor must catch that. Others, perhaps half of all visits in Malaysian GP clinics, come only for a medical certificate for a minor ailment they could treat themselves. The doctor has to stay alert to the emergency while the queue is full of people who want a signature. It is easy to feel invalidated when you are worried about a patient's health and the patient only wants an MC.

## What the demands do to a doctor

He put numbers on the time problem. A safe consultation needs ten to fifteen minutes. A clinic seeing a hundred patients over a ten-hour day needs two doctors; one doctor alone gives each patient six to eight minutes. That is before the complexity of the case is counted, and before the extra work of an emergency department or a government clinic, where each encounter also includes treatment, injections, blood taking and a second review once results return. The cognitive, emotional and physical load on that doctor can be two or three times higher.

The physical hazards are plain. In a busy hospital the doctor is always standing or walking; in a GP clinic the doctor sits in a small room for a ten-hour shift and goes home with backache. In a government clinic the doctor cannot leave the station and holds off going to the toilet because the queue does not stop. Cognitively, every patient demands a decision and a check on that decision, and the load accumulates: the first ten patients of a shift get an attentive doctor, the last ten get an exhausted one. From the patient's side that is a hazard too, because the care you receive depends on when in the doctor's shift you arrive.

Long hours erode family and social life. Doctors tend to have no circle outside medicine, so they go home and talk about patients. Burnout from the clinic gets vented at home as short temper or withdrawal. His view is that most burnt-out government doctors do not need anything elaborate; they need time to recover.

He manages his own exposure by choosing where and how long he works. If a clinic is very busy, he limits himself to a short shift. He is clear that this is a luxury that comes from experience and from having other work, and that most doctors in the government sector cannot choose their load and keep going out of a sense of duty to the public, at the cost of burnout.

## Working alone, and what support looks like

A freelance GP answers only to the clinic owner and the profession's guidelines, which is simple. The trade-off is working alone: one doctor in the building, nobody to discuss a case with, and emergencies handled with whatever competence the clinic assistants have. For some doctors that is fine; others perform better in a team and should seek an emergency department or a multi-doctor clinic.

The clinic he describes as supportive is a small one: the owners, a handful of long-serving assistants, and a few locum doctors who trust each other. The owner does not micromanage prescribing or how the doctor speaks to patients, lets the doctor pray or eat when there is no patient, and backs the staff when a patient turns abusive. He contrasted that with clinics where staff refuse to assist or do not trust the doctor, and with the wider pressure on GP clinics from Google reviews and complaints, which pushes many owners to manage for patient expectations rather than for good care.

## What he recommends

A doctor performs best when three conditions hold: they feel safe, they have room to grow, and their own needs are met. He uses the image of a plant that needs oxygen, sunlight and soil, and stops growing when any one is missing.

Safe means not being made to work in a state that invites mistakes, and not fearing unfair repercussions for baseless complaints. From that follow his specific recommendations:

- Boundaries on time: a doctor who has worked past their physical limit is given rest, and seeing ten patients an hour is not treated as better than seeing five.
- Fair scheduling: on-call rosters that respect family life and treat everyone equally.
- Pay by the hour actually worked: if existing doctors are asked to absorb rising numbers instead of the organisation hiring more, the extra hours are paid, or they become a source of burnout. He noted that private practice paid him for extra hours where government service expected them for free.
- Psychological support that takes burnout seriously instead of normalising it. He pointed to aviation, where pilots' hours and rest are tightly regulated because one mistake endangers everyone aboard, and asked why the same logic does not apply to doctors.
- Autonomy in career paths: Malaysian doctors are pushed along a single track from house officer to specialist, and those who are not given a training place often resign because there is nowhere else to go. Allowing sabbaticals, letting specialists locum privately, and giving fair access to training places would keep more of them.

His closing advice to doctors was to know their limit, and to remember that stepping back to look at other places is allowed. Having worked in several settings, he knows where he wants to be, and he hopes other doctors get the chance to find the same.
